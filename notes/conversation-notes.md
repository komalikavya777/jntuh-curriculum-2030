# Conversation record

**Who:** A recent M.Tech graduate (also completed B.Tech CSE), now working as a software
engineer in industry.

**Format:** in person  conversation, 15 September 2026.
## What they said

1. Deployment, and writing production-ready, maintainable, secure code, were learned
   almost entirely through work — not through B.Tech or M.Tech.

2. Data Structures & Algorithms, Databases, and Computer Networks seemed simple during
   undergrad but turned out to matter a lot in practice. By contrast, Operating Systems,
   Theory of Computation, Compiler Design, and Engineering Mathematics felt more important
   academically at the time but have had limited day-to-day relevance since.

3. M.Tech research added value — taught them to dig deeper and approach problems
   systematically — but most of the daily technical foundation still comes from B.Tech
   fundamentals, not the research-level work.

4. AI has meaningfully improved their productivity: it helped them understand a large,
   unfamiliar company codebase quickly, and now handles much of the routine coding and
   speeds up code review — freeing them to focus on architecture, technical decisions, and
   risk management.

5. Practical exposure to production deployments, the SDLC, testing methodologies, and
   Git/version control would have made their first year at work significantly easier.

6. What they actually had to learn on the job wasn't technical at all: working and
   learning consistently through changing team conditions, managing time to meet
   deadlines, and critically self-reviewing their own work.

## What I did not expect

Point 6. I expected the "unexpected" answer to be about a missing technical subject —
instead, the thing they named as hardest to learn on the job was not a course topic at
all: working under shifting team conditions and learning to critically self-review. No
curriculum change I've proposed touches this, and it's not the kind of gap a syllabus
credit can obviously fix.

## Where this agrees and disagrees with my report

**Agrees strongly with:** the Git/version control gap (Section 6, addition 1), the
production-deployment/testing/CI gap (Section 6, addition 5, Software Practice), and
directly supports the Verification & Evaluation course's core premise — point 4 describes
almost exactly the (a)/(b) pattern my own tests found: AI handles the routine artefact,
the human's real job is judgement, review, and risk.

**Complicates, rather than confirms, one of my own labels:** point 2 names Theory of
Computation (FLAT) and Compiler Design as subjects that felt important academically but
had limited day-to-day relevance — which sits in tension with my own KEEP/UPDATE argument
for these subjects in Section 3. I address this directly in Section 7a.
